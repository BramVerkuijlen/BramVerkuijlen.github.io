Background & Context

This recommendation originates from the early research phase of the Monitoring Expansion internship project, in which the feasibility of building a modern observability pipeline was explored.

Initially, the project aimed to investigate a complete, end-to-end observability architecture using modern tooling such as OpenTelemetry, Dataprepper, OpenSearch, and Grafana. The intention was to evaluate how such a pipeline could be applied to enterprise Content Management Systems and how it would compare to existing monitoring solutions in use at AmeXio.

As the project progressed, it became clear that fully researching, designing, and validating an entire observability pipeline was not feasible within the available timeframe. As a result, the scope was refined, and the project pivoted toward a focused Proof of Concept (POC) that concentrated on extracting meaningful telemetry — primarily performance-related data — from OpenText Content Management Server (xECM).

This shift led to a strong emphasis on:

  • Agentless telemetry collection

  • Log-based performance metrics

  • API-based availability and timing checks

  • Exploring the practical limits of observability in Windows-based enterprise ECM environments

While the POC does not aim to replace existing monitoring solutions, its findings naturally raise questions about how this newly discovered telemetry could be used in practice.

Within AmeXio, AMI (AmeXio Monitoring Insights) is the primary platform used to collect, process, visualize, and act upon monitoring data across customer environments. As such, AMI became a natural point of comparison and an important reference when evaluating the relevance and applicability of the POC results.

The recommendations in this section therefore focus specifically on AMI:

  • How the findings of the POC relate to AMI’s current architecture

  • Where the POC complements existing AMI capabilities

  • Which limitations become apparent when introducing new types of telemetry, such as performance metrics

  • How AMI could evolve over time to better accommodate modern observability practices

It is important to note that these recommendations are partially validated through hands-on implementation in the POC and partially exploratory in nature. Architectural evolution, platform modernization, and long-term roadmap decisions could not be fully researched or implemented during the internship and are therefore presented as informed directions rather than finalized designs.

This section does not propose replacing AMI, nor does it suggest an immediate or disruptive overhaul. Instead, it aims to provide a grounded, experience-based perspective on how AMI could gradually evolve while continuing to support existing systems, customers, and operational workflows.

Current State of AMI (Architecture & Operating Model)

This section describes the current architecture and operating model of AMI (AmeXio Monitoring Insights) as it is understood within the scope of this project.
The goal is to document how AMI functions today, not to evaluate or judge design decisions.

High-Level Architecture Overview

AMI is a custom-built monitoring platform developed and maintained by AmeXio.
It is designed to collect, process, store, visualize, and alert on telemetry data originating from customer environments, with a strong focus on OpenText-based systems such as:

  • OpenText Content Management Server

  • OpenText Documentum

  • Other enterprise content platforms

AMI consists of multiple tightly integrated components, combining both open-source tooling and custom-developed services.

At a high level, the AMI architecture includes:

  • Custom monitoring collectors deployed on client systems

  • A centralized ingestion and processing pipeline

  • Multiple storage backends for telemetry and reporting

  • Visualization and alerting components

  • A feedback mechanism to continuously refine alert behavior

Data Collection Model

AMI primarily relies on custom-built collectors that run on monitored systems.

These collectors:

  • Are tailored to specific platforms or products

  • Collect telemetry in predefined formats (commonly XML-based)

  • Focus on availability, health, and known failure scenarios

  • Are often developed and maintained in-house

  • Provide business information using API calls and database queries

The strong coupling between collectors and specific systems allows AMI to capture domain-specific signals, but also means that:

  • Each new system or telemetry type requires collector development

  • Maintenance effort increases as systems evolve

  • Adding new metric types (e.g. performance or latency metrics) can be complex

Ingestion and Processing Pipeline

Collected telemetry is forwarded to the AMI backend, where it enters a processing pipeline based on Logstash.

Within this pipeline:

  • Incoming telemetry is filtered and categorized

  • Filters are driven by rules stored in a PostgreSQL database

  • Known or previously classified events can be:

    • Ignored

    • Marked as informational

    • Escalated as alerts

This filtering approach effectively acts as a blacklist-based model, where:

  • Unknown or new events are surfaced

  • Known, accepted events are filtered out or downgraded

This mechanism helps reduce noise but also directly influences which data is ultimately retained.

Storage and Visualization

After processing, telemetry data is stored in an InfluxDB time-series database.

From there:

  • Grafana is used for visualization and dashboarding

  • Dashboards focus on system health, availability, and known indicators

  • Data is primarily numeric and structured according to AMI’s internal models

AMI also includes business reporting functionality, which:

  • Extracts data from InfluxDB

  • Generates higher-level reports for operational or business stakeholders

Alerting and Feedback Loop

AMI uses a custom alerting implementation rather than relying solely on standard alerting tools.

Key characteristics include:

  • Alerts are triggered based on processed telemetry

  • A custom web service allows operators to:

    • Mark alerts as valid

    • Acknowledge or dismiss issues

  • These actions update the PostgreSQL-based filter rules

  • Future similar events are then handled according to the updated rules

This creates a feedback loop where operational knowledge directly influences future alert behavior.

Operational Characteristics

Based on this operating model, AMI exhibits several defining characteristics:

  • Strong domain knowledge embedded in collectors and filters

  • Tight coupling between data collection, filtering, and alerting

  • A focus on reducing alert noise through pre-filtering

  • A reliance on custom components that require ongoing maintenance

This architecture has proven effective for its intended purpose, particularly in environments with well-understood systems and known failure modes.

At the same time, the introduction of new telemetry types — such as high-cardinality metrics, performance timings, or trace-like data — introduces challenges that were encountered during the Monitoring Expansion POC and are discussed in the following sections.

Recommendations – Evolution of AMI

This section provides recommendations for the future evolution of AMI (AmeXio Monitoring Insights), based on the findings of the Monitoring Expansion Proof of Concept (POC).

The recommendations combine:

  • Hands-on technical findings from the POC

  • Architectural observations of the current AMI platform

  • Exploratory ideas that could not be fully implemented within the project timeframe

The goal is not to propose an immediate replacement of AMI, but to outline a realistic, phased path toward a more flexible and future-proof observability platform.
https://grafana.com/blog/opentelemetry-best-practices-a-users-guide-to-getting-started-with-opentelemetry/

Position AMI as an Evolving Observability Platform

AMI has grown into a powerful monitoring solution tailored to AmeXio’s client landscape, particularly OpenText-based systems. Its strength lies in:

  • Deep domain knowledge of enterprise ECM platforms

  • Custom collectors designed for specific OpenText products

  • Established operational workflows for alerting and reporting

It is therefore recommended to position AMI as an evolving observability platform, rather than a fixed monitoring solution. This mindset allows AMI to adapt gradually while maintaining support for existing clients.

Observability Alignment Considerations

Modern observability systems are generally built around the following principles:
https://grafana.com/blog/opentelemetry-best-practices-a-users-guide-to-getting-started-with-opentelemetry/

  • Collect first, filter later

  • Preserve raw telemetry for future analysis

  • Favor flexible querying over static alert definitions

  • Use standardized telemetry formats

The current AMI architecture makes different trade-offs.

Current AMI Characteristics

AMI currently relies on:

  • Custom-built collectors emitting telemetry in a proprietary XML format

  • Early-stage filtering on client systems to reduce network traffic

  • Central filtering via Logstash using rules stored in PostgreSQL
    https://www.elastic.co/logstash

  • A blacklist-style alerting approach, where known issues are filtered out over time

This design works well for:

  • Known systems

  • Known failure patterns

  • Controlled environments with predictable telemetry

Observability Implications

While effective, this approach introduces limitations when compared to modern observability practices:

  • Telemetry is filtered before storage, reducing auditability
    https://grafana.com/blog/opentelemetry-best-practices-a-users-guide-to-getting-started-with-opentelemetry/

  • New metrics or performance data require collector changes

  • Retrospective analysis is difficult if data was filtered out

  • Introducing new telemetry types increases maintenance overhead

These are not design flaws, but historical trade-offs made to manage network traffic, storage constraints, and operational complexity at the time AMI was designed.

The POC highlights that newer tooling can address many of these challenges more flexibly.

Incremental Modernization Using Open Standards

Rather than replacing AMI, it is recommended to incrementally modernize the platform by introducing open standards and community-maintained tooling where possible.

Key recommendations:

  • Gradually replace custom collectors with:

    • OpenTelemetry Collectors
      OpenTelemetry

    • Fluent Bit or similar open-source agents
      Fluent-bit

  • Use configuration-driven collectors instead of custom code

  • Allow community tooling to handle protocol updates and new telemetry types

This reduces:

  • Long-term maintenance effort

  • Dependency on custom collector development

  • Risk when introducing new monitoring use cases

Backend, Scaling, and Network Considerations

One of AMI’s core challenges is managing network traffic and data volume, which historically led to aggressive filtering.

Modern approaches can mitigate this:

  • Use batching and compression in collectors
    https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-collector/blob/main/processor/batchprocessor/README.md

  • Deploy horizontally scalable backends (e.g., Loki, OpenSearch)
    https://grafana.com/docs/loki/latest/operations/scalability/ https://forum.opensearch.org/t/understanding-opensearch-scaling/18438

  • Scale collectors dynamically using container orchestration (e.g., Kubernetes)
    https://kubernetes.io/ https://opentelemetry.io/docs/collector/deployment/gateway/

  • Host backend components in the cloud to scale on demand
    https://www.geeksforgeeks.org/cloud-computing/cloud-scalability/

An alternative model—validated by the POC—is to:

  • Keep telemetry storage close to the client

  • Allow centralized Grafana access across multiple environments
    https://grafana.com/grafana/dashboards/

  • Provide per-client dashboards while reducing centralized ingestion pressure

This approach also aligns with client feedback received during the 2025 XDay event.

Strategic Outlook

In summary:

  • AMI remains valuable and relevant

  • Its current architecture reflects valid historical decisions

  • Modern observability tooling offers opportunities to:

    • Reduce maintenance

    • Improve flexibility

    • Expand monitoring capabilities

The Monitoring Expansion POC provides a safe experimentation platform to guide these decisions [link to POC home page].

A phased, standards-based evolution allows AmeXio to:

  • Protect existing investments

  • Adopt new monitoring capabilities

  • Stay aligned with modern observability practices [link to observability principles article]
